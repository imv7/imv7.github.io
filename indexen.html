Three years, and no hurry

I went to Thailand for a meditation retreat and stayed three years. What held me was not the technique but the context: the knowledge, the rhythm, the routine of the temple, the quiet of the morning, care for the body as part of a whole life.

I studied with different teachers, in different cities.

An hour has a route

The traditional sequence begins at the feet and rises. There is a reason for it: the body accepts touch from the ground upwards more readily. Touch a number to see each moment.

  1. 1. Conversation — A few minutes on sleep, pain, surgeries and anything you would rather I did not touch.
  2. 2. Wai khru — The silent opening gesture, in respect to the teachers. It marks where the session's time begins.
  3. 3. Feet and legs — Face up, with palm and thumb pressure along the inner and outer lines.
  4. 4. Hips and abdomen — Hip openings and light work on the belly, always following the breath.
  5. 5. Back — Face down, pressure using body weight along the spine, without impact or abrupt manoeuvres.
  6. 6. Arms and hands — Forearm, wrist and each finger, an area nearly always forgotten by people who type all day.
  7. 7. Neck and head — Seated or lying down, with gentle traction and points at the base of the skull.
  8. 8. Stretches and rest — Assisted stretches come at the end, once the tissue has already yielded. Then a few still minutes.

    Where it comes from

    The name

    Nuad means to touch with pressure. Bo-Rarn means ancient. The usual translation loses that second half.

    Wat Pho

    In the nineteenth century, King Rama III had the diagrams of lines and points carved in stone in Bangkok. A public medical library.

    Shivago Komarpaj

    Tradition reveres Jivaka Kumar Bhaccha, a physician contemporary with the Buddha, as its founding figure. His portrait presides over almost every classroom in Thailand.

    Metta

    The practice was born inside temples and keeps the Buddhist intention of metta, loving kindness. Without it, technique is only applied force.

    Recognition

    In 2019 UNESCO inscribed Nuad Thai on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

    No oil, clothed

    The traditional session takes place on a floor mat, with the person dressed in light clothing.

    Before a first session

    Do I need to undress?
    No. Traditional Thai massage is done clothed, in light comfortable clothing, without oil, on a floor mat.
    Does it hurt?
    The pressure is firm and adjusted to you. Say at any point if you want it lighter, and I adjust straight away.
    How long does it take?
    A full Nuad Bo-Rarn session lasts one hour. The seated chair version lasts fifteen minutes.
    Do I need to be flexible?
    No. The stretches are assisted and go as far as your body goes that day, not one degree further.
    Where do you work?
    In the Federal District, Espírito Santo and Chiang Mai, depending on where I am. Home visits arranged by message.
    Can I learn it?
    Yes. I teach the fundamentals to people who already practise and want to refine their technique, and to complete beginners.
